# Structural ideation and the folding archive

Use the core methods first (`../nazar-core/references/ideation.md`). The ones below are specific to structure. Aim for **one structural move that means something**, not a gimmick: the move should come from the brand idea, the product's behaviour, or the place it comes from.

## Methods
1. **Start from the gesture.** List what a person does with the product (pour, pinch, share, gift, hide, refill, show off). Then design the opening around the most telling gesture. A pack for something shared might open from the middle; a pack for something secret might open from underneath.
2. **Deform the cube.** Paul Jackson's method in *Structural Packaging* (2012): take a square-cornered box and move one thing at a time (bevel an edge, twist the top, curve a crease, lift a corner, add a gusset) and see what it becomes [SRC]. Sketch 30 deformations in an hour, folded in paper, not drawn.
3. **One sheet, no glue.** The constraint that produced the oyster pail, the pizza box and most origata. It forces locks and folds to do the work, and it's usually cheaper on the line.
4. **Borrow a mechanism.** Pop-up engineering (V-folds, parallel folds, pull tabs), the matchbox drawer, the flip-top hinge lid, the tetrahedron, the egg tray's cells, the book's spine, the accordion. Transplant one into a category where it's never been used.
5. **Structural metaphor.** The shape stands for the idea: a tear-off strip that "breaks the seal" of something new, a pack that unfolds like a map for a travel brand, a window cut like the brand's mark. Test: can you say the metaphor in one sentence without the logo?
6. **Second life.** What does the pack become once empty: a holder, a planter, a game, a frame, a refill station? Only keep it if people would actually do it.
7. **SCAMPER on structure:** Substitute (material), Combine (two packs into one), Adapt (a mechanism from furniture, fashion, architecture), Modify (scale, a curve, a ratio), Put to other use, Eliminate (the lid, the glue, the insert), Reverse (open from the bottom, print inside).
8. **Kill list:** structures that only work in renders; flaps that shoppers tear; "clever" opens that need instructions; anything a filling line can't run at speed (unless the brand accepts hand-filling and its cost).

## The archive: where structural ideas come from
Look here before any trend gallery. Log what you found and why it matters in `research/structure/`.

### Japanese wrapping (tsutsumi) and ceremonial folding (origata)
- **Hideyuki Oka, *How to Wrap Five Eggs*** (1967, with photographs by Michikazu Sakai; sequel *How to Wrap Five More Eggs*, 1975): traditional Japanese packs in straw, bamboo, leaves, wood and paper, collected across Japan [SRC]. Study how the material decides the closure and how a pack shows what's inside.
- **Origata:** ceremonial paper folding for wrapping and gifting, separate from recreational origami. **Ise Sadatake's** treatise (often called *Hoketsuki* / *Tsutsumi-no Ki*, 1764) codified formal wrappers and knots [SRC]. **Yamaguchi Nobuhiro** (Origata Design Institute) reinterprets origata for modern life [SRC]. The Met holds an *Origata tehon* (models of paper folding) [SRC].
- **Furoshiki:** a single cloth that wraps any shape, the ultimate reusable pack.
- Lesson: wrapping as respect. The fold *is* the message, and the way it opens is part of the gift.

### South Asian forms (desi archive)
Kaif's taste dial. Use these as grammar, never as costume [KNOWN unless tagged]:
- **Mithai box:** thin board, a lid that tucks or slides, often tied with string and stamped with the shop's name: the shop's identity carried home on a string.
- **Paan dan:** a compartmented metal box (*dan* = container) with separate wells for each ingredient; the Royal Collection holds a pair from Indore, c. 1870–75 [SRC]. The idea: a ritual organised by compartments.
- **Gilori (folded paan):** a leaf folded into a triangle and pinned with a clove, a closure that is also an ingredient.
- **Newspaper cones and bags (puriya / thonga):** street snacks in recycled paper folded into a cone or glued into a bag. Maximum reuse, zero brand cost, instant recognition.
- **Dona and pattal:** leaf bowls and plates stitched with twigs: compostable before the word existed.
- **Kulhad:** an unglazed clay cup, used once and returned to the earth.
- **Tiffin carrier (dabba):** stacked tins locked by one handle: modular and portable.
- **Potli:** a drawstring cloth pouch for gifts and dry fruit.
- **Matchbox labels:** a drawer and sleeve, plus a century of tiny graphic covers (Tasveer Ghar's matchbox label collection on Google Arts & Culture) [SRC: see core sources].

### Western and industrial canon
- The **egg carton** (cells that hold fragile forms), the **Chinese takeout oyster pail** (one sheet, a wire handle), the **Tetra Pak tetrahedron** (fewest material for the volume), the **flip-top hinge-lid** carton, the **pizza box** (the 0427 family), the **triangular chocolate prism**, the **Toblerone-type** shape as a trademark [KNOWN].
- **Museum of Brands** (Robert Opie's collection, London, collected since 1963) for how packs evolved and what shoppers kept recognising [SRC].
- **Cooper Hewitt** packaging collection for designed objects, including structures [SRC: core sources].
- **Curved-crease folding** (David Huffman's research; contemporary paper artists): curved creases make shells, not boxes. The pillow pack is the everyday version.

## From research to routes
1. 50+ paper folds and thumbnails, clustered into 3–5 territories (gesture, metaphor, mechanism, reuse, place).
2. Three routes: **standard code** (cheap and solid), **modified standard** (one move), **unorthodox** (a new structure), each with the one-sentence reason, rough cost (die, board area, filling speed) and risk.
3. A paper sample of each, photographed on a plain ground for the presentation. No 3D renders unless they come from a real CAD model of the real dieline.
