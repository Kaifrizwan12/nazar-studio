# Shelf research

## Go to the shelf (or get photos of it)
- Photograph the real shelf in 3–5 stores (premium, mass, local), including the blocks of colour, the price tags, the lighting and the clutter.
- Note the **eye level** and what's placed there, facings, how products are stacked (front-facing, spine-out, hanging), and the shelf strips and wobblers.
- Buy the top competitors and handle them: material, weight, opening, the smell of the ink, what's on the back.

## Category codes
Make a sheet of what the category "says" visually: dominant colours, imagery style, type styles, claims, structures. Mark each code:
- **Keep** (it signals the category, and shoppers need it to find the product), e.g. a colour that means "low fat"
- **Break** (it's a cliché, and breaking it makes us visible)
- **Own** (a code nobody uses well, which we can make ours)

## Findability vs visibility [SRC]
- **Findability:** can a regular buyer find *our* product fast? It depends on consistent distinctive assets (colour, shape, brand block).
- **Visibility:** does it catch the eye of someone not looking for it? It depends on contrast with the neighbours, scale and simplicity.
Research from pack-testing firms links the most visible pack designs to far stronger sales from shelf, so test both, early.

## The competitor line-up test
Mock up the shelf: your concept among 6–10 real competitors, at real size (print them or composite at true scale). View it:
- at 2–3 m, in greyscale, and in a 2-second glance. Is the brand found? Is the variant clear?
- up close: does the pack reward a second look (detail, story, a giggle)?

## Online shelf
For e-commerce, the pack is a **thumbnail** first: test it at 150–300 px, on white, next to competitors' listing images. The brand and variant must read at that size.
